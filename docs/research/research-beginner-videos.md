# Research: beginner-investing reference videos

Source for Networth's tone and teaching order. Everything below is paraphrased from YouTube captions (auto-generated). No long quotes.

Key for the video columns:
V1 Warikoo (Hindi, long) | V2 Mark Tilbury "Investing for Beginners" | V3 Humphrey Yang "Stock Market for Beginners" (long, US) | V4 Mark Tilbury "How I Pick My Stocks" | V5 Sanjana Short "How to start investing in stocks" (India, Hinglish) | V6 Nidhi Kunwar Short "Stock, SIP, Mutual Fund, ETF, everything simplified" (India, Hindi, about 108 s)

## 1. Per-video summaries

**V1 Warikoo (Hindi).** A talking-head, step-by-step "what do I need before I start" walk-through for nervous first-timers. Starts by separating delivery investing (hold) from intraday trading (same-day), says the second is time-hungry and greed-driven, then lists the prerequisites: PAN, demat, a broker, emergency fund, insurance. Uses one memorable rule-of-thumb (Rule of 72) with rupee numbers to show doubling time. Easy because it is conversational, in the viewer's own language, and answers the fear ("will I lose money?") before the mechanics. Caveat: the caption is badly garbled, so detail below is gist only.

**V2 Tilbury "Investing for Beginners".** Opens with a story (one person investing since 1985 through three crashes) so the lesson "time in the market" lands emotionally before any jargon. Explains index funds with a music-charts analogy, then shows the actual phone taps to open an account, deposit, auto-invest a small daily amount (priced as one coffee), and place an order. Easy because every abstract idea is tied to a daily-life object and then to a screen.

**V3 Humphrey Yang (US, long-form).** A structured course: why invest, how much you can make, vocabulary, categories, what to buy, account types, how to research, portfolio building, live app demos, taxes. Easy because it states the agenda up front, uses worked dollar examples (compounding, a 30-year projection), and defines each term at the moment it is needed. Also includes opinionated picks and a sales pitch, see section 4.

**V4 Tilbury "How I Pick My Stocks".** A personal checklist: three simple ratios from the company's statements, then qualities (brand, news hype, leadership, future industries), then dollar-cost averaging. Easy because each ratio comes with a pass mark and a worked example, and the dating and smoothie-shop analogies make accounting feel human. Heavily "here is how I choose", so most of it is out of bounds for Networth (section 4).

**V5 Sanjana Short (India).** A roughly one-minute, four-beat plan: how much, open a demat, research, do not panic. Playful voice (the "closet" analogy for demat, dating metaphors for risk), ends with a single small action and a comment-to-get-more hook. Easy because it gives only one idea per beat and one next step. Crosses the line on "invest 10%" and broker names.

**V6 Nidhi Kunwar Short (Hindi).** Explains stock, mutual fund, ETF and SIP in under two minutes through one running story: a company that wants to grow sells small slices of itself. Each product is then defined as a variation on the last (a basket of companies, then the same basket traded like a stock, then SIP as a way of paying, not a product). Easy because of the single thread, ordering from simple to complex, and the explicit "SIP is not a product" clarification. Caveat: ends with a "best option for teens" claim, see section 4.

## 3. Teaching patterns worth copying in short chat messages

1. **One thread, one idea, one next step.** V6 runs a single "company sells slices" story and builds stock, fund, ETF and SIP from it. V5 gives a four-beat plan and ends on a single action. A WhatsApp reply should carry exactly one idea and offer one follow-up.
2. **Ladder of definitions.** Define the new thing as "like the previous thing, except X" (ETF = fund that trades like a stock). Keeps each message to two or three sentences.
3. **Everyday object analogies.** Music charts for an index, a closet for a demat account, a basket for a mutual fund, buckets and cups for diversification, supermarket price tag vs haggling at a market for market vs limit order, airport codes for tickers, a first date for reading financial statements. Pick local ones for India (kirana, chai, auto fare).
4. **Small round numbers, shown twice.** Compounding: 1,000 at 10% becomes 1,100 then 1,210. Averaging: three monthly prices and the resulting average. Doubling: Rule of 72 with Rs 1 lakh. Always show the arithmetic in one line, then the takeaway in one more.
5. **Price the habit as a coffee.** Daily Rs 5 (one coffee) makes a tiny recurring amount feel painless. For India use a chai or auto ride.
6. **Order of ideas that worked:** why bother (inflation, idle cash) then what a stock is then risk and diversification then product types then how to start (account) then do not panic. Safety-net first (emergency fund, insurance) appears in V1 and V2.
7. **Fear first.** Open with the worry (will I lose money, is it too late) and answer it with time, not with tips (V1, V2, V3).
8. **Remember-it hooks.** Bulls have horns pointing up, bears have claws swinging down. Cheap mnemonics survive in a chat.
9. **Compare within a group.** "A ratio only means something against similar companies." Good template for explaining any number.
10. **Disclaimers inline, short.** V2 and V3 say once, in plain words, that this is not personal advice and returns can fall. Keep it to one clause.
11. **Hinglish register (V5, V6).** English for terms (stock, ETF, SIP, risk), Hindi for connecting words and feelings. Mirror the user's language each turn.
12. **Short-format (Shorts) notes.** About 100 to 150 spoken words. A hook in the first sentence that names who it is for. Beats are numbered or clearly sequenced. A joke or relatable aside per beat to keep attention. A concrete "do one thing" close. A soft call to action (reply with a word to get more). Strip nuance rather than compress it, and say "ask me for the details" to push depth into the next turn. Danger: brevity encourages absolute claims (best, safe, at least 10 percent), which Networth must not copy.

## 4. Advice that crosses Networth's line (do NOT copy)

Networth never says what to buy or sell, or how much to allocate.

**V1 Warikoo**
- Age-based allocation rule (a share of monthly savings, roughly 75 percent at age 25, to equities, the rest to safer options). That is an allocation instruction.
- Suggests going into a Nifty 50 index fund, with mid-cap and small-cap funds for those wanting more risk, and a minimum holding of about 5 years.
- Names a particular broker as number one in India, and mentions specific companies in examples (captions are garbled so unclear which).
- Quotes a target return around 12 percent, framed as a plan.

**V2 Tilbury "Investing for Beginners"**
- Pushes a specific broker app plus a promo code and free share (sponsored).
- Recommends the S&P 500 index fund, accumulation class, and a daily Rs-equivalent auto-invest.
- Demonstrates buying a named single stock (Tesla, an amount) after saying beginners should not.
- Dismisses inflation worry ("as long as you invest, not a worry") and shows 31 to 40-year projections as if expected. Present such figures only as illustration with a risk note.

**V3 Humphrey Yang**
- Recommends specific brokers, a Roth IRA as the single takeaway, a specific three-fund split (60/30/10), named ETFs and tickers, and a dividend portfolio of five named stocks.
- Walks through his own Robinhood thesis and says he bought and is up, then promotes a paid community of his trades. That is a stock pick plus a funnel.
- Quotes average returns of 8 to 10 percent and "just keep buying" as universal rules.
- Treats US tax rules as general. Many do not apply to India.

**V4 Tilbury "How I Pick My Stocks"**
- Pass marks (current ratio above 1, operating margin above 15 percent) used as screening rules, with Apple, Tesla, Coca-Cola as examples of good picks.
- Promotes free-stock links from two apps.
- Predicts sector shifts and says a particular company's leader risk is a strength and weakness.
- Safe to copy: the concept of what a ratio measures, never the pass/fail thresholds as advice.

**V5 Sanjana Short**
- "Invest at least 10 percent of income in equities" (allocation).
- Names brokers (Zerodha, Dhan) as recommended.
- Suggests moving to mid-cap or small-cap to "increase risk exposure", and "research the hell out of a company" with a metric list. Fine as concept, not as an instruction to buy.
- Offers a DM'd "starter plan" via comment-bait.

**V6 Nidhi Kunwar Short**
- States mutual funds are the "best and safe" option for teenagers and calls risk low. Absolute safety claim and a product recommendation.
- Ranks ETF as riskier than a mutual fund and less risky than a stock, a generalisation to avoid stating as fact.
- Suggests starting from very small amounts (Rs 100 to 500); the amount may be quoted as "many apps allow small starts", not as advice to invest it.

**Also avoid (all videos):** guaranteed or implied return numbers as forecasts, "start young" urgency as pressure, affiliate or sponsored framing, "just keep buying no matter what", "safe" or "best" labels.
